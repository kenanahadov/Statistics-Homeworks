Penetration scores: LLN and CLT

Simulate attackers across systems and compare total, average and square-root-normalized scores.

1. Model and experiment

Each of m attackers attempts n systems in sequence. The score at system i is Xᵢ. Increments are independent and identically distributed within each trajectory; attackers are also independent repetitions of the same process.

  • Bernoulli: Xᵢ = 1 with probability p and 0 otherwise. Sₙ counts one attacker's successful penetrations.
  • Rademacher: Xᵢ = +1 or −1, each with probability ½. This represents progress or a setback, giving a net score with mean zero.

Total: Sₙ = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ Xᵢ   ·   Average: Sₙ/n   ·   Normalized total: Sₙ/√n
Write μ = E[Xᵢ] and σ² = Var(Xᵢ). Bernoulli: μ = p, σ² = p(1−p). Rademacher: μ = 0, σ² = 1.

Bernoulli totals have the binomial distribution: P(Sₙ = k) = C(n,k)pᵏ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. The coefficient C(n,k) counts the paths with k successes, as on the board. For Rademacher, Sₙ = 2B−n with B ∼ Binomial(n,½).

The experiment records scores at n = 10, 100, 1,000 and, if selected, 10,000. Checkpoints use the same trajectories as they grow. At each checkpoint, the m scores form a dataset; counting scores in intervals produces the displayed distributions.

4. Law of Large Numbers

The average converges in probability to μ: to p for Bernoulli, and to 0 for Rademacher. The lecture's concentration around p concerns the Bernoulli success fraction.

For every fixed ε > 0: P(|Sₙ/n − μ| > ε) → 0.
Chebyshev's inequality: P(|Sₙ/n − μ| > ε) ≤ min(1, σ²/(nε²)).

As n grows, the probability of an average far from μ tends to zero. This does not require monotone improvement of one path or of a finite simulated table. Zero observed exceptions does not prove zero probability. The bound concerns the true probability; a sample proportion can exceed it by chance.

5. Central Limit Theorem

For Rademacher, E[Sₙ/√n] = 0 and Var(Sₙ/√n) = 1 for every n. As n increases, its distribution approaches N(0,1). Switch the distribution display to Rademacher to compare the normalized histograms with the normal curve.

Rademacher: Sₙ/√n ⇒ N(0,1).
Bernoulli, 0 < p < 1: (Sₙ − np)/√(np(1−p)) ⇒ N(0,1).

For Bernoulli, the requested uncentered Sₙ/√n has mean p√n and variance p(1−p), so it does not converge to the standard normal. Centering and variance standardization are needed. At p = 0 or 1, Bernoulli is deterministic.

The CDF gap is the largest difference between the empirical cumulative distribution and the standard normal CDF, including both sides of each jump. It measures shape, not just mean and variance. The normal CDF uses a numerical approximation (error below 10⁻⁷). This is a descriptive comparison, not a hypothesis-test p-value. Finite m adds sampling noise, so the gap need not decrease in every run.

6. Systems, attackers and conclusion

More systems: Bernoulli's expected total is np, while its success fraction becomes more concentrated around p. For Rademacher, the expected total remains zero but its standard deviation grows as √n. Its average approaches zero even though the total can be large, positive or negative.

More attackers: m supplies more independent observations of the same score distribution, improving the stability of histograms and estimates. It does not change an individual attacker's law. With m = 2,000, a measured proportion has standard error at most 1/(2√m) ≈ 1.12 percentage points.

Aggregate over all attackers: A = Σₐ₌₁ᵐ Sₙ⁽ᵃ⁾.
E[A] = mnμ; Var(A) = mnσ²; Var(A/(mn)) = σ²/(mn).

Conclusion: totals spread, averages concentrate, and square-root scaling preserves variance. In the symmetric zero-mean model it also reveals a standard normal limit. The simulations illustrate these theorems; they do not prove them. Real attacks may involve shared vulnerabilities, learning and unequal success probabilities, which this i.i.d. model excludes.
